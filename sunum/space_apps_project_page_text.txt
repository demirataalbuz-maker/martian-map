PROJECT NAME
==================================================
Marswalk Map

==================================================
HIGH-LEVEL SUMMARY
==================================================
Marswalk Map is a web globe and route planner for landing and moving safely on Mars. The user picks a crewed or a robotic mission. The globe first screens the whole planet with NASA elevation, thermal inertia, albedo, radiation shielding and dust storm data, and shows why Jezero crater is a good, data-rich study site. Inside Jezero, the tool shows where the chosen lander can touch down, suggests the nearest suitable landing site for a clicked science target, and plans the fastest safe route for a suited astronaut, a crewed rover or a robotic rover. Every point of the route is checked against life support, walk-back, energy and distance limits, and the result is a clear GO / CAUTION / NO-GO decision. Every limit is traced to a NASA or mission source.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION
==================================================
**What it does.** Marswalk Map answers two questions a Mars mission must solve before anyone steps out: where can we land, and how far can we safely go from there?

1. **Globe screening.** We dressed the whole of Mars with MOLA elevation, TES thermal inertia and TES albedo. A landing screen combines elevation, latitude and the thick-dust test (thermal inertia below 100 and albedo above 0.25). It leaves about 48% of the surface open for a crewed lander (below +2 km, within ±50°) and 18% for a robotic lander (below +0.5 km, within ±30°). We also show atmospheric shielding from radiation, based on the dose and pressure relation measured by Curiosity's RAD, and dust storm frequency from five Mars years of MRO MARCI storm maps. Jezero passes every test: about 70% of Mars has a thinner air shield, and 82% is stormier. Jezero also has the most ready-made terrain data, so it is our study area.
2. **Two scales.** The crewed scenario uses an 86 × 107 km area at 20 m, built from 64 USGS CTX terrain models. The robotic scenario uses the USGS 1 m HiRISE terrain model of the delta area (about 20 × 21 km), sampled at 5 m, because a remotely driven rover needs fine detail.
3. **Landing maps.** For each cell, the steepest slope inside the lander's safe circle decides suitable, marginal or not landable. Sand dunes and ripples from the USGS geologic map are marked as hazards.
4. **Routes.** A* finds the fastest route over a cost grid where slope, ground and vehicle limits set the speed. At every point we check four things: the astronaut could still walk back if the vehicle fails (6.4 h of an 8 h suit), the battery can still bring the vehicle home with a 20% reserve, the base is no more than 10 km away, and the total mission time stays inside the suit time budget. Where a check fails, the failing part is drawn in dashed red with a "!" marker and the reason. Long routes are split into EVAs or sols, with a STOP marker at the end of each.
5. **Honest data.** Each layer shows its source and its known limits. CTX terrain resolves features of about 75–80 m (we measured this against HiRISE), so boulders are not seen. Suit and vehicle limits come from NASA lunar requirements, because no official crewed Mars rover limits exist.

**How we built it.** We used only ready-made NASA and USGS products; we did not process raw stereo images. We blended the 64 CTX terrain models with NASA Ames Stereo Pipeline. We corrected each model's vertical offset with USGS quality files and found and fixed a 32–85 m horizontal shift by matching each model's own orthoimage. Checked against HiRISE, the remaining shift is 0–12 m. Processing used GDAL, QGIS, Python, NumPy and SciPy. The site runs fully in the browser: TypeScript, Vite, MapLibre GL, with routing in a Web Worker and no server.

**Why it matters.** Landing site and traverse planning are usually separate studies. Marswalk Map puts them in one view, for both crewed and robotic missions, with sources a reviewer can check.
